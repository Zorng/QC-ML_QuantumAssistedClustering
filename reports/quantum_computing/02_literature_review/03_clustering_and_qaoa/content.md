### Graph-based clustering and QAOA

Clustering groups similar samples without using their class labels. A similarity graph represents each sample by a vertex and gives each pair a nonnegative weight $w_{ij}$: a larger weight indicates greater similarity, and an absent edge has weight zero. A cut separates the vertices into two groups; its weight is the sum of weights crossing between them. Minimizing this cut tends to keep strongly similar samples together. Lucas describes an Ising formulation of minimum graph partitioning with an equal-size penalty for an even number of vertices [[1]](ref.md#lucas2014-ising). The project's weighted, soft-balance objective below adapts that idea; it is a modeling choice rather than a result established by that paper.

Let $x_i\in\{0,1\}$ assign sample $i$ to a group, $n$ be the number of samples, and $\lambda\geq 0$ be the balance-penalty weight. The objective to minimize is $C(x)=\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}(x_i-x_j)^2+\lambda(\sum_i x_i-n/2)^2$. The first term counts a weight exactly when its endpoints have different labels; the second penalizes deviation from half the samples in each group. Maximizing the cut of positive *similarity* weights would instead reward separating similar samples. By contrast, Otterbach *et al.* discuss maximizing a cut weighted by distances, so a large crossing weight represents dissimilarity [[2]](ref.md#otterbach2017-unsupervised). Their experiment on a 19-qubit processor used weighted MaxCut instances; their Euclidean-distance clustering example was simulated. This illustrates a hybrid approach, but does not validate the present objective or establish a practical speedup.

The penalty is soft: it trades cut weight against group size, rather than enforcing two nonempty groups. If $\lambda$ is too small, assigning every sample to one group can minimize the objective; at $\lambda=0$ it has zero cost for nonnegative weights. When $n$ is odd, the closest possible group sizes differ by one and their minimum balance contribution is $\lambda/4$, not zero. A large penalty can favor equal sizes even when the natural groups are unequal. Similarity construction and the value of $\lambda$ remain open design choices; no value is confirmed here.

### From binary objective to quantum cost

A quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) expresses a cost as a constant plus terms in individual bits and pairs of bits. Because $x_i^2=x_i$, define $d_i=\sum_{j\ne i}w_{ij}$ and write $C(x)=\lambda n^2/4+\sum_i[d_i+\lambda(1-n)]x_i+\sum_{i<j}2(\lambda-w_{ij})x_i x_j$. Each pair occurs once in this upper-triangular convention. The constant matters when reporting the original objective even though it cannot change the minimizing assignment.

For a quantum representation, let $Z_i$ be the Pauli-Z operator on qubit $i$, with $Z|0\rangle=+|0\rangle$ and $Z|1\rangle=-|1\rangle$. Thus a measured bit $x_i$ corresponds to eigenvalue $z_i=1-2x_i$, or $x_i=(1-z_i)/2$. Replacing these eigenvalues by operators gives the diagonal cost Hamiltonian $H_C=(\frac12\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}+\lambda n/4)I+\sum_{i<j}(\lambda-w_{ij})Z_iZ_j/2$. A Hamiltonian here is an operator whose eigenvalue on a computational-basis assignment equals its cost; its lowest-energy assignments minimize $C$. There are no single-$Z$ terms in this symmetric formulation. The constant contributes only a global phase to the cost circuit, which does not change measurement probabilities, but must be restored for energies and objective gaps.

### QAOA and interpretation of results

Farhi, Goldstone, and Gutmann introduce QAOA as alternating parameterized cost and mixer operations from a uniform superposition, with a classical search over parameters [[3]](ref.md#farhi2014-qaoa). For $n$ assignment qubits, the initial state is $|+\rangle^{\otimes n}$, where $|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$. The standard mixer is $H_B=\sum_i X_i$, with $X_i$ the Pauli-X bit-flip operator. In each layer $\ell=1,\ldots,p$, apply the cost operation $U_C(\gamma_\ell)=e^{-i\gamma_\ell H_C}$ and then the mixer $U_B(\beta_\ell)=e^{-i\beta_\ell H_B}$. The real parameters $\gamma_\ell$ and $\beta_\ell$ govern the transformations, while $p$ is the number of layers. Repeated preparation and measurement provide sampled assignments whose costs estimate the expected cost; a classical optimizer adjusts the parameters to minimize that expectation.

The original paper formulates its examples as maximization [[3]](ref.md#farhi2014-qaoa). Here the same exponential convention is paired with minimization of $H_C$; equivalently one could maximize $-C$ with consistently adjusted signs. Sign and parameter conventions must be documented when comparing implementations. Increasing $p$ enlarges the ideal variational family, but a finite-depth run and a practical parameter search do not guarantee the global optimum. The original theoretical analysis concerns particular MaxCut cases, not this weighted clustering objective.

The optimized expected cost summarizes the distribution, whereas the cost of a returned assignment is computed from one selected measured bit string. The most frequent string need not have the lowest cost. A proposed policy is to report the lowest-cost sampled assignment with two nonempty groups, along with its cost, the expected-cost estimate, and sample counts; if none is sampled, report an invalid result rather than inventing a partition. This selection policy is separate from the objective and remains to be agreed. Each sample requires one logical assignment qubit; feature values are used classically to construct weights, so reducing feature dimensions alone does not reduce that count. Small simulations can check the mapping and circuit behavior, but do not demonstrate practical quantum advantage. Optimizer selection and noise evaluation are separate review dependencies.
