Clustering and similarity graphs provide the classical foundation for the quantum-assisted approach. The representation chosen for the samples determines the relationships given to a graph algorithm, while classical clustering methods provide useful comparisons. This subsection explains those choices and their implications for the two-group graph objective developed in the following subsection.

### Clustering and the K-Means baseline

Clustering is an unsupervised task: it groups samples according to their measured features without using known class labels to create the groups. A sample is one observation, and a feature is a measured property. Group labels are arbitrary; exchanging the names of two groups leaves the partition unchanged. Similarity depends on the representation and the question being studied, so a single clustering need not capture every meaningful pattern.

K-Means takes a chosen number of groups and seeks to minimize the sum of squared Euclidean distances from samples to their assigned centroids, the mean feature vectors of their groups. It repeatedly assigns samples to their nearest center and updates the centers. K-Means++ chooses initial centers randomly with a preference for points far from existing centers. Arthur and Vassilvitskii analyze this seeding method and its expected cost, but a particular run need not find the global optimum [[1]](ref.md#arthur2007-kmeanspp). Its centroid-based objective differs from the project's graph-cut objective. K-Means is therefore a baseline for comparing clustering outcomes, rather than an exact solver for that objective; it may also struggle with groups whose shapes are not well described by compact regions around centers.

### From distances to a similarity graph

The input to the graph can be the scaled features or a lower-dimensional representation produced by PCA. Feature scaling and dimensionality reduction change distances and may change which samples appear close. Let $z_i$ and $z_j$ denote the chosen feature vectors for samples $i$ and $j$; their Euclidean distance is $d_{ij}=\|z_i-z_j\|$. A small distance means the points are close, whereas a large similarity means their connection is strong. A graph represents samples as vertices and similarities as nonnegative edge weights.

One candidate is the radial basis function (RBF) weight $w_{ij}=\exp(-d_{ij}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))$ for distinct vertices, with $w_{ii}=0$. Here $\sigma>0$ controls how quickly similarity decreases with distance. For identical coordinates the weight is one, and it decreases as distance increases. The width must be interpreted in the chosen feature space; changing scaling or PCA changes the meaning of the same numerical width. A fully connected RBF graph gives every distinct pair a positive weight in exact arithmetic. Von Luxburg discusses this and neighborhood-based constructions as alternative similarity graphs [[2]](ref.md#vonluxburg2007-spectral-tutorial).

Sparsification retains only selected relationships. For example, each sample may select its nearest neighbors, but that choice can be one-sided. An undirected graph can retain an edge if either endpoint selects the other, or only if both do; these rules need not produce the same graph [[2]](ref.md#vonluxburg2007-spectral-tutorial). Neighbor counts, ties, edge weights, and the symmetrization rule must be recorded. Sparsification can create disconnected components, which may reflect group structure or an overly restrictive graph. An isolated vertex has no positive-weight edges and provides no similarity evidence for assigning it to either group. Its weighted degree is zero, so methods requiring inverse degrees need an explicit handling policy. The kernel, width, neighborhood rule, and treatment of isolated vertices remain open project choices.

### Spectral clustering and the quantum graph objective

Spectral clustering derives a new representation of samples from a matrix built from their similarity graph, then clusters the transformed samples. In the method of Ng, Jordan, and Weiss, the graph weights are normalized using vertex degrees, leading eigenvectors of the normalized similarity matrix form an embedding, and its rows are normalized before a K-Means step [[3]](ref.md#ng2001-spectral-clustering). Their analysis and examples concern a particular algorithm and assumptions; they do not guarantee good partitions for every graph. A zero-degree vertex makes the usual inverse-degree normalization undefined and must be handled explicitly. Spectral clustering uses graph relationships to form its embedding; PCA instead obtains directions from feature covariance. Although both use eigenvectors, they answer different questions and can appear at different stages of the same pipeline.

The project's proposed quantum objective uses the graph to seek a two-group assignment that discourages cutting strong similarity edges while softly preferring similar group sizes. This size preference can distort naturally unequal groups, and it does not guarantee two nonempty groups for every penalty setting. It is not the K-Means objective or the spectral method's embedding-and-clustering procedure. Meaningful comparison therefore uses the same samples and documented preprocessing, reports the graph and group sizes, and evaluates the resulting partitions under shared conditions.
