Preprocessing determines the data representation used by the clustering pipeline. This subsection examines classical and quantum approaches to principal component analysis, focusing on their inputs, outputs, and practical requirements. It also considers how their results could be compared and made usable for the subsequent construction of a similarity graph.

### Classical PCA and data representation

Principal component analysis (PCA) replaces the original features with directions along which the data vary most, as reviewed by Jolliffe and Cadima [[1]](ref.md#jolliffe2016). Keeping only a few directions gives each sample fewer coordinates, but may discard information useful for clustering. Scaling changes the relative influence of features; centering subtracts their means. The project intends to scale before PCA and record the fitted transform. Classical and quantum branches must use the same samples, centering, scaling, and retained dimension when directly compared.

Let $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ contain the scaled, centered samples as rows, with $n\geq2$. The feature covariance is $S=X^\mathsf{T}X/(n-1)$. Its eigenvectors are feature-space directions and the corresponding eigenvalues describe variance. Classical PCA obtains the leading $k$ directions as columns of $V_k$ and produces the classical score matrix $Z=XV_k$. The graph stage can calculate distances between rows of $Z$. Retaining variance is not itself evidence of better clusters; changing coordinates can change graph weights. The sample Gram matrix $G=XX^\mathsf{T}/(n-1)$ instead describes relationships between samples. It shares the nonzero eigenvalues of $S$, but its eigenvectors live in sample space. If $Gu=\lambda u$ and $\lambda>0$, the corresponding unit feature direction is $v=X^\mathsf{T}u/\sqrt{(n-1)\lambda}$. Zero eigenvalues cannot be used in this recovery formula.

### Quantum PCA and input assumptions

Lloyd, Mohseni, and Rebentrost describe quantum PCA for a density matrix: a positive semidefinite, trace-one operator representing a quantum state. Their method uses repeated copies of that state to approximate evolution generated by the density matrix and applies phase estimation to access eigenvalues and eigenstates in quantum form [[2]](ref.md#lloyd2014-qpca). Its input access and quantum output differ from a classical table of PCA scores. Preparing data, resolving the spectrum, and reading results must therefore be included when evaluating an application to this project.

For a nonzero centered matrix, a suitable feature-space encoding is $\rho=X^\mathsf{T}X/\|X\|_F^2=S/\operatorname{tr}(S)$, where $\|X\|_F^2$ is the sum of squared entries. It has the same feature eigenvectors as $S$, with eigenvalues divided by $\operatorname{tr}(S)$. One possible preparation samples nonzero row $x_i$ with probability $\|x_i\|^2/\|X\|_F^2$ and prepares the amplitude-encoded state $|x_i\rangle=x_i/\|x_i\|$; their mixture is $\rho$. Equal probabilities for normalized rows generally produce a different matrix, because they discard row norms. A zero row has zero weight and cannot itself be normalized. If all entries are zero, $\rho$ is undefined and no principal direction is identifiable. Features with zero variance also need an explicit scaling policy; division by zero must be avoided.

State preparation is not free: loading the classical rows and their norm-based probabilities, padding a feature register to a power of two where needed, and preparing many copies add cost. Lloyd *et al.* assume access to suitable quantum states or data-access operations [[2]](ref.md#lloyd2014-qpca). Their density-matrix exponentiation uses copies of $\rho$ in controlled evolution. A different small demonstration could instead construct $\rho$ and a matrix exponential classically, then supply controlled operations to a quantum phase-estimation circuit. The latter is a quantum eigenvalue-estimation experiment with classical operator construction; it must not be reported as implementing density-matrix exponentiation or as providing the original method's resource scaling.

Quantum phase estimation uses controlled powers of a unitary and an inverse quantum Fourier transform to estimate its eigenphase [[3]](ref.md#cleve1998-quantum-algorithms). With $U=e^{i\pi\rho}$, a normalized covariance eigenvalue $r\in[0,1]$ produces phase $r/2$, avoiding the alias between zero and one that would arise at a full $2\pi$ scaling. An input with overlap on several eigenvectors produces a distribution of phase outcomes; components with no input overlap cannot be observed. A finite phase register resolves only approximate phases, and nearby eigenvalues can be difficult to separate. A phase measurement alone yields neither a list of feature axes nor the score matrix required for the similarity graph.

Li *et al.* experimentally investigate a different, resonance-based quantum PCA method on a small quantum state and use tomography to examine a principal component [[4]](ref.md#li2021-resonant-qpca). This is evidence for a specific small quantum-state experiment, not an end-to-end classical-data clustering pipeline. For the proposed pipeline, obtaining numerical feature axes from conditional quantum states would require repeated measurements in suitable bases and classical reconstruction, including relative signs or phases. Merely measuring computational-basis probabilities loses sign information. Once a consistent real basis is reconstructed, classical multiplication by $X$ yields scores. The costs and errors of sampling, postselection, reconstruction, and projection must be reported; a circuit returning only eigenvalues is insufficient.

### Candidate demonstration and comparison

A proposed first demonstrator uses a small centered two-feature dataset with nonzero, separated eigenvalues. Construct $\rho$ and $U=e^{i\pi\rho}$ classically, run quantum phase estimation on a one-qubit feature register initialized as an equal mixture of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, and estimate the phases from repeated measurements. This input overlaps every feature direction. Conditional measurements in multiple bases can reconstruct a leading eigenstate when the phase outcomes are sufficiently distinguishable. Recover a real direction up to sign, compute the projected scores classically, and check that graph construction receives them. This is a proposal, not a completed implementation; the QPE circuit must actually produce the conditional state, rather than loading a classically calculated eigenvector as the purported result.

Validate estimated eigenvalues and the retained subspace against classical linear algebra, and record precision, success counts, uncertainty, input and operator construction, all registers, gate resources, and timing boundaries. A global phase or sign does not change a direction or pairwise distances. Equal eigenvalues allow rotations within their eigenspace, so compare projectors or principal subspaces rather than individual vectors. If degeneracy crosses the chosen $k$ boundary, the leading $k$-dimensional subspace is itself not unique; report the ambiguity rather than forcing an agreement. Small eigengaps, zero total variance, and inadequate phase resolution need explicit failure or uncertainty handling. Neither this simulated demonstrator nor the cited theoretical and small-scale experimental work establishes a practical quantum speedup for classical tabular clustering.
